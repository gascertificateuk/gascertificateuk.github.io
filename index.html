What is a gas safety certificate (CP12)?

A gas safety certificate, known as a CP12, is the record of the annual gas safety check that landlords in England must arrange for every rented property with gas appliances. It is not a service and not a repair — it is the paperwork that proves a Gas Safe registered engineer inspected the gas appliances, pipework and flues, and found them safe to use on the day of the check.

The law behind it is the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998. The landlord's duties are simple to state and easy to get wrong in practice: arrange the check every 12 months, use a Gas Safe registered engineer, keep the certificate for at least two years, and give a copy to the tenant within 28 days of the check — or before they move in, for a new tenancy.

The certificate itself lists each appliance tested, the result, any defects found and what was done about them. If something is unsafe, the engineer will either fix it on the spot or mark it as needing attention before the appliance is used again. A certificate is only ever a snapshot of the day it was issued. It does not guarantee the boiler will behave for the next twelve months — that is what the next check is for.

What the check covers

A CP12 check covers every gas appliance the landlord owns in the property, the pipework that feeds them, and the flues that carry fumes away. In a typical rented flat that means the boiler, the gas hob or cooker, and any gas fire.

For each appliance the engineer checks that it is burning gas correctly, that the flue is clear and properly fitted, that ventilation is adequate, and that safety devices work as they should. They test the gas pressure, check for leaks on the pipework, and look at the physical condition of the appliance — corrosion, damage, or parts that have been fitted wrongly.

The engineer also checks the things tenants never see: the gas meter and emergency control valve, and the pipework running through the property. A tightness test shows whether gas is escaping anywhere in the system. If the engineer finds an appliance that is "immediately dangerous", they will disconnect it and tell the landlord and tenant why. If it is "at risk", they will explain what needs doing and by when.

What the check does not cover: the tenant's own appliances, such as a cooker the tenant brought with them, unless the tenancy agreement says otherwise. It also does not cover the central heating system's water side — radiators, for example — beyond what affects gas safety.

How often, and the 2-month early renewal rule

A CP12 is due every 12 months, and the deadline is strict: the new certificate must be in place before the old one expires. There is no grace period. But there is a useful rule that most landlords miss — the two-month early renewal rule.

If the new check is carried out within two months of the old certificate's expiry date, the new certificate keeps the original expiry date. A certificate that expires on 30 June can be renewed any time from 1 May, and the new one will still run to 30 June the following year. The landlord loses no time at all by booking early.

That matters because access is the most common reason checks run late. Tenants go on holiday, change their number, or simply do not answer. Booking the check six to eight weeks early gives room to chase access, rearrange a missed visit, and still land the certificate before the deadline. It also avoids the situation every landlord dreads: realising on the expiry date that nobody has booked anything.

Keep the certificate for at least two years, and give the tenant their copy within 28 days. If a tenant moves in mid-year, they get their copy before they move in.

What it costs

There is no fixed price for a CP12. The cost depends on how many appliances there are, where the property is, and whether anything needs fixing during the visit. As a rough guide, a single-appliance check in London costs around £60, and a typical two-appliance property — boiler plus hob — costs more. Gas Certificate's own pricing starts at £29 for a basic check.

What the price should include: the visit itself, the testing of every landlord-owned gas appliance, the tightness test on the pipework, and the certificate. If the engineer finds a fault, the repair is usually charged separately — ask before the visit what is included and what is not.

Be wary of prices that look too good to be true. A check that takes ten minutes is not a check. A proper CP12 on a two-appliance property takes 30 to 60 minutes. If the engineer is in and out in a quarter of an hour, something was skipped.
